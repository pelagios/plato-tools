Your files stay on your computer, and nothing is sent to any other site unless you allow it. A dataset of a million places has been checked and converted in this page; how large a dataset your browser can take, and what changes on the command line, which also takes many files at once, is in the guide.

The tools

  • Elenchos

    Check

    Put your data to the test: every problem found, named and pointed to, before anyone else relies on it.

    Choose →
    MoreLess about Elenchos

    Checks a file against PLATO's own definitions: a JSON document against PLATO's JSON Schemas, the spreadsheet tables against their table definitions, and linked data against the terms the ontology defines. It also finds mistakes no schema can, such as a route that, through its members, is a member of itself.

    The report comes in three parts: problems to put right, each named in plain words with where it is (the sheet, row and column, or the place); warnings worth a look; and what would not be carried over if you converted the file.

    Checking, in the guide

  • Metaphrasis

    Convert

    Move your data between PLATO JSON, RDF, the spreadsheet tables and Linked Places Format, and be told exactly what each format cannot carry.

    Choose →
    MoreLess about Metaphrasis

    Converts between PLATO JSON (and JSON Lines), linked data, the spreadsheet tables and Linked Places Format. Linked data is read as N-Triples, N-Quads or Turtle, and written as N-Triples.

    Between PLATO JSON and linked data nothing is lost. The tables and Linked Places Format hold less, by design: the report names everything left out, and where leaving out a detail would change what a statement says, as with a denial or a withdrawn statement, the whole statement is left out, and the report says so.

    Converting, in the guide

  • Arithmos

    Statistical figures

    Figures from census volumes and statistical tables, published as RDF Data Cube and checked against its rules.

    Choose →
    MoreLess about Arithmos

    For figures from census volumes, returns and other statistical tables. PLATO keeps each figure with its address in the table (the county, the occupation, the sex), so that a place is never said to “have” an occupation.

    Choosing Arithmos sets the conversion to N-Triples with the RDF Data Cube option: each figure becomes a qb:Observation, with its place and date as refArea and refPeriod, and a date that is not one year or one day is reported, not guessed. On the command line, datacube then runs Data Cube's integrity checks on the result.

    Statistical tables, in the guide

  • Mneme

    Version check

    Published is published. Compare two versions of your dataset and show that nothing was quietly changed or lost.

    Choose →
    MoreLess about Mneme

    Once a dataset is published, its attestations are only ever added to: a correction is a new attestation that withdraws or replaces the old one, which stays. Choose the new version, then the earlier one; they may be in different formats, since what is compared is what each attestation says.

    A problem is an attestation missing, changed, or with its web address lost or altered; for the first few, the report shows what changed. A place or source described differently is a warning, since those may be corrected.

    Comparing two versions, in the guide

  • Hermes

    Readers

    Bring in the places you linked in Recogito, with the regions you marked on georeferenced maps, the places named in a TEI edition, and any spreadsheet (CSV) or GeoJSON you already have.

    For regions on a map, choose the Recogito export together with the map's georeference from Allmaps (and its IIIF manifest, if you have it). A CSV or GeoJSON has its columns matched to PLATO before converting.

    Choose →
    MoreLess about Hermes

    Reads what you already have into PLATO, to check, convert and add to:

    • Recogito's annotations: each place you linked becomes an attestation, citing the document and where in it.
    • Regions you marked on a map in Recogito, placed as points when you choose the export with the map's georeference from Allmaps; each cites the map and the georeference.
    • The place names in a TEI edition, EpiDoc included, that point at a gazetteer.
    • Any CSV or GeoJSON of places, its columns guessed and matched to PLATO's fields, for you to correct before converting.

    In the guide: annotations from Recogito, place names in a TEI edition, your own table of places

  • Agora

    Publish

    From checked to citable: what your dataset still needs to be FAIR, and its deposit files; a permanent address for every attestation; a web page for every place; and the w3id rules.

    Choose →
    MoreLess about Agora

    Prepares a dataset for publishing, in four parts, in order:

    1. Report: what its description still lacks to be FAIR (a title, authors with ORCIDs, a licence, a base address that will last), and the deposit files for a repository that gives it a DOI.
    2. Mint: a copy in which every attestation has a permanent address of its own.
    3. Site: a website for GitHub Pages, with a page for each place.
    4. w3id: the rules that send your w3id.org addresses to that site.

    Nothing is uploaded: it writes files, and publishing them is for you to do.

    Publishing your dataset, in the guide

  • Chora

    Place on the map

    See each place on the map, with everything its attestations say of it over time. Draw a point, a line or an area as a new attestation.

    Opens the map on its own page, with your file if you have chosen one ↗
    MoreLess about Chora
    • Every place on the map, and, for the one you choose, what its attestations say of it over time.
    • Search by any name a place has, not only the one it is listed under (“Byzantium — also Konstantinoupolis”).
    • Draw a point, a line or an area as a new attestation, with you named as its contributor.
    • Save the whole dataset as PLATO JSON, offered only once the version check has found nothing deleted or changed.
    • Large datasets: the map shows up to 50,000 places, and finds the rest by name.
    • Other basemaps than this site's own, used only once you allow them in the Permissions panel.

    Coming next: lay a georeferenced historical map (a IIIF image, georeferenced with Allmaps) over the basemap, and trace places from it by hand; what you trace cites the map and the georeference that places it.

    Placing on the map, in the guide

  • Krisis

    Match review

    Is this Newton that Newton? Find the same places in another dataset, weigh each suggested match, and keep your judgement on the record.

    Choose →
    MoreLess about Krisis

    Matches your dataset with another, in any format PLATO tools reads. Places whose names are alike, and that are near each other, are suggested, and you decide each: the same place, not this one, or different places, saying why.

    Only your decisions go into the data, as attestations recording you as the one who made them. Save the review to carry on later; at the end, take your dataset with the new attestations added, checked by the version check, or the new attestations alone. Matching two files on your computer sends nothing anywhere.

    Coming next: looking your places up in the World Historical Gazetteer, only if you allow it, for the same review.

    Reviewing matches, in the guide
